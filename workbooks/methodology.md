### PUB-0023 — Why Organizational Culture Is Designed, Not Declared

- **Status:** FINAL / PUBLISHED contribution; methodological interpretation is a contribution record.
- **Methodological contribution title:** Culture as a System Outcome
- **Question or problem examined:** How can organizational culture be examined beyond declared values, statements, or surveys?
- **Analytical sequence / model:** System Design → Repeated Experience → Behaviour → Cultural Pattern
- **Key dimensions or decision points:** Decision rights; responsibility and accountability; information access and movement; treatment of mistakes; incentives and rewards; approval and escalation patterns; leadership practices; repeated exceptions; unwritten expectations; normalized behaviour.
- **Practical application:** Examine what organizational systems repeatedly cause people to experience and do. Where stated values and recurring behaviour diverge, investigate possible system-design causes rather than assuming the issue is solely a people problem.
- **Limitations / conditions of use:** This is a diagnostic lens, not a claim that every cultural pattern has a single system-design cause. Other relevant context should not be excluded.
- **Methodological principle:** To understand culture, examine the behaviour the system repeatedly reinforces.
- **Relationship to existing SARGEN™ methodology:** Connects system design and repeated behaviour to organizational culture without introducing a separate locked framework.
- **Cross-links:** [Publications](publications.md) — PUB-0023; [Identity](identity.md) — PUB-0023; [Philosophy](philosophy.md) — PUB-0023.
- **Open questions or review notes:** Preserve the distinction between declared culture and repeatedly reinforced behaviour. No separate locked status for this methodological contribution has been recorded.

### PUB-0024 — Why Every Organization Needs Institutional Learning

- **Status:** FINAL / PUBLISHED contribution; methodological interpretation is a contribution record.
- **Methodological contribution title:** Institutional Learning as a System Capability
- **Question or problem examined:** What has an organization changed because of what it experienced?
- **Analytical sequence / model:** Experience → Reflection → Learning → System Change → Future Action
- **Key dimensions or decision points:** Decisions; processes; rules and principles; documentation; training; context; continuity.
- **Practical application:** Ask, “What is different now because of what happened then?” Examine whether experience influences future decisions and processes, whether useful reasoning is preserved, whether context remains understandable, and whether learning survives changes in people, roles, teams, and leadership.
- **Individual vs institutional learning:** Individual learning improves a person through experience. Institutional learning changes organizational action or capability and preserves useful learning beyond the individuals who experienced the event.
- **Methodological principle:** Experience becomes institutional learning when it changes future organizational action and the learning is preserved beyond the people who experienced it.
- **Limitations / conditions of use:** A visible change alone does not prove that learning was sound or that the change improved outcomes. The reasoning, context, and results may also require review.
- **Relationship to existing SARGEN™ methodology:** Connects institutional memory and continuity with the conversion of experience into future organizational action.
- **Cross-links:** [Publications](publications.md) — PUB-0024; [Identity](identity.md) — PUB-0024; [Philosophy](philosophy.md) — PUB-0024.
- **Open questions or review notes:** This is a diagnostic method, not a claim that every experience must result in a process change; the appropriate response depends on what the experience reveals.

### PUB-0025 — Why Complexity Is the Hidden Cost of Growth

- **Status:** FINAL / PUBLISHED contribution; methodological interpretation is a contribution record.
- **Methodological contribution title:** A Diagnostic Chain for Growth-Driven Complexity
- **Question or problem examined:** What has organizational growth changed within the system, and is governance evolving at the same pace as complexity?
- **Analytical sequence / model:** Growth → Complexity → Dependencies → Decision Points → Risk → Governance Pressure
- **Key dimensions or decision points:** New dependencies; new decision points; coordination difficulty; less visible risks; responsibility clarity; areas requiring stronger governance.
- **Practical application:** Trace the organizational consequences of growth instead of assessing growth only by scale, revenue, people, geography, or activity. Identify complexity and its consequences, then govern them deliberately.
- **Methodological objective:** Understand Complexity → Identify Its Consequences → Govern It Deliberately.
- **Limitations / conditions of use:** The chain is a diagnostic progression, not a universal claim that every instance of growth produces the same risks or governance needs. Complexity is not itself a problem to eliminate.
- **Relationship to existing SARGEN™ methodology:** Extends system analysis toward dependencies, decision demands, risk, and governance pressure.
- **Cross-links:** [Publications](publications.md) — PUB-0025; [Identity](identity.md) — PUB-0025; [Philosophy](philosophy.md) — PUB-0025.
- **Open questions or review notes:** Examine whether governance arrangements evolve in response to the complexity actually created.

### PUB-0026 — Why More Rules Do Not Always Create Better Governance

- **Status:** FINAL / PUBLISHED contribution; methodological interpretation is a contribution record.
- **Methodological contribution title:** From Rule Accumulation to Governance Capability
- **Question or problem examined:** Is an organization responding to complexity mainly by accumulating rules, or by developing broader governance capability?
- **Analytical sequence / model:** Growth → Complexity → Governance Pressure → Rules → Governance Capability
- **Key distinction:** Rules are defined expectations, requirements, restrictions, or controls. Governance is the wider system through which authority, decisions, accountability, boundaries, and adaptation are structured.
- **Key dimensions or decision points:** What problem a rule addresses; who has authority under it; what decision boundary it establishes; who is accountable; whether it operates consistently; whether it can adapt when the system changes; and whether it improves governance or merely adds another layer of control.
- **Practical application:** Evaluate the contribution of rules to governance capability rather than using rule count as a proxy for governance quality.
- **Limitations / conditions of use:** Rules remain valid and sometimes necessary governance instruments. The method does not imply that fewer rules automatically produce better governance.
- **Relationship to existing SARGEN™ methodology:** Continues PUB-0025's examination of governance pressure by distinguishing governance instruments from the broader capability needed to govern a system.
- **Cross-links:** [Publications](publications.md) — PUB-0026; [Identity](identity.md) — PUB-0026; [Philosophy](philosophy.md) — PUB-0026.
- **Open questions or review notes:** The sequence is a diagnostic progression; rules are one possible response to governance pressure, not the whole of governance.

### PUB-0027 — Why Decision Boundaries Matter More Than More Approvals

- **Status:** FINAL / PUBLISHED contribution; methodological interpretation is a contribution record.
- **Methodological contribution title:** From Approval Layers to Decision Boundaries
- **Question or problem examined:** Who should decide, within what boundary, and with what accountability?
- **Analytical sequence / model:** Decision → Authority → Boundary → Escalation → Accountability
- **Key distinctions:** Approval is a mechanism of authorization or review. Authority is the legitimate ability to make a decision. A decision boundary is the defined limit within which a decision can be made without escalation. Accountability is responsibility for the consequences and outcome of the decision.
- **Key dimensions or decision points:** Authorized decision-maker; limits of authority; conditions for escalation; risks defining the boundary; accountability; alignment between authority and responsibility; review of boundaries as conditions change.
- **Practical application:** Examine whether approval arrangements appropriately locate authority and accountability, or create unnecessary upward movement. Determine where independent decisions are appropriate and where review or escalation is required.
- **Limitations / conditions of use:** Approval is not inherently undesirable and may be appropriate where risk, law, policy, or organizational responsibilities require it. Fewer approvals do not automatically mean better governance.
- **Relationship to existing SARGEN™ methodology:** Develops PUB-0026's governance-capability concern into the structure of actual decision authority and boundaries.
- **Cross-links:** [Publications](publications.md) — PUB-0027; [Identity](identity.md) — PUB-0027; [Philosophy](philosophy.md) — PUB-0027.
- **Open questions or review notes:** Preserve the distinction between approval and authority; do not treat them as synonyms.

### PUB-0028 — Why AI Needs Governance

- **Status:** FINAL / PUBLISHED contribution; methodological interpretation is a contribution record.
- **Methodological contribution title:** From AI Capability to Governed System Participation
- **Question or problem examined:** How do AI capability, permitted authority, human oversight, intervention, and accountability operate together within an organizational decision system?
- **Analytical sequence / model:** Capability → Authority → Boundary → Oversight → Intervention → Accountability
- **Key dimensions or decision points:**
  1. **Purpose and role:** What purpose the AI serves and whether it recommends, supports, or executes actions.
  2. **Authority and boundaries:** What it can recommend or execute, what information and resources it can access, what remains outside its authority, and what requires review or escalation.
  3. **Human oversight:** What a person must review, whether outputs can be assessed and challenged, and whether the person can reject, override, or stop operations.
  4. **Consequence and risk:** Error consequences, reversibility, appropriate oversight, and proportionality of controls to capability and permitted authority.
  5. **System-level governance:** Surrounding processes, exceptions, monitoring, accountability, and change governance over time.
- **Practical application:** Evaluate AI as a participant in a wider operating system, not solely by model accuracy or the presence of a human reviewer. Examine permissions, decision boundaries, practical intervention mechanisms, and responsibility.
- **Methodological objective:** Align AI capability with appropriate authority, oversight, and accountability.
- **Limitations / conditions of use:** The appropriate controls depend on context, permitted authority, and potential consequences. A nominal human reviewer does not by itself establish meaningful oversight.
- **Relationship to existing SARGEN™ methodology:** Applies decision-boundary and accountability concerns to AI participation within organizational systems.
- **Cross-links:** [Publications](publications.md) — PUB-0028; [Identity](identity.md) — PUB-0028; [Philosophy](philosophy.md) — PUB-0028.
- **Open questions or review notes:** Preserve the distinction between what a system can do and what it is authorized to do.

### PUB-0029 — Why Automation Cannot Replace Judgment

- **Status:** PUBLISHED contribution; methodological interpretation is a contribution record.
- **Methodological contribution title:** From Automated Execution to Contextual Judgment
- **Question or problem examined:** Does the defined process remain appropriate under the circumstances in which it operates?
- **Analytical sequence / model:** Process → Conditions → Boundary → Intervention → Accountability
- **Core examination:**
  1. **Process:** What task or decision has been defined?
  2. **Conditions:** Do current circumstances match the assumptions of the process?
  3. **Boundary:** Where does the process remain sufficient, and where does judgment become necessary?
  4. **Intervention:** When should execution pause, escalate, or require human assessment?
  5. **Accountability:** Who remains responsible for the decision and its consequences?
- **Methodological principle:** Automation should operate within defined boundaries. When circumstances fall outside those boundaries, the system must have an appropriate path for judgment, intervention, or escalation.
- **Practical application:** Examine automated systems beyond execution speed and efficiency. Review operating conditions, process assumptions, exceptions, decision boundaries, intervention pathways, escalation, and accountability.
- **Relationship to PUB-0028:** PUB-0028 examines governed AI participation, including capability, authority, oversight, and accountability. PUB-0029 examines when automated execution is no longer sufficient for the circumstances and a path for contextual judgment or intervention is required. Automation is broader than AI; the contribution should not be limited to AI systems alone.
- **Limitations / conditions of use:** Not every exception requires human intervention. The system should define proportionate paths appropriate to the context, risks, reversibility, and consequences.
- **Cross-links:** [Publications](publications.md) — PUB-0029; [Identity](identity.md) — PUB-0029; [Philosophy](philosophy.md) — PUB-0029.
- **Open questions or review notes:** Publication ID PUB-0029 does not establish an article sequence number. Do not infer Article-29 or Article-30 from the publication ID. Publication metadata is maintained in the Publications workbook and canonical Publication Register.

### PUB-0031 — Why People Leave but Systems Shouldn’t

- **Status:** FINAL / PUBLISHED contribution; methodological interpretation remains a contribution record.
- **Methodological contribution title:** Preserving Essential Knowledge and Responsibility Through Human Change
- **Phase / series:** Phase-6 — Human System; Gateway
- **Publication date:** 31 Oct 2026
- **Platform:** Medium
- **Public link:** https://medium.com/@sargen.india/why-people-leave-but-systems-shouldnt-f872f0f594b4
- **Question or problem examined:** How can an institution sustain essential knowledge, responsibilities, and learning when the people who carry them change or leave?
- **Analytical sequence / model:** Human Contribution → Identify Essential Knowledge → Clarify Role and Responsibility → Transfer Context and Ownership → Govern Access and Review → Adapt Through New Judgment
- **Key dimensions or decision points:**
  1. **Essential knowledge:** Identify what the institution cannot afford to lose; do not assume every experience must be recorded.
  2. **Role and person:** Distinguish formal responsibilities from the experience, judgment, and relationships contributed by an individual.
  3. **Transfer and continuity:** Preserve relevant context, clarify ownership, and provide reasonable handover and access arrangements.
  4. **System limits:** Recognize that documents and procedures cannot transfer every aspect of experience or remove the need for judgment.
  5. **Governance and review:** Determine relevance, access, ownership, confidentiality, and when inherited practices should be revised or discontinued.
  6. **Adaptation:** Carry learning forward without making previous decisions permanently authoritative.
- **Practical application:** Examine roles and critical processes for dependencies on individual memory or presence. Identify essential knowledge and responsibilities, establish proportionate transfer and review practices, and preserve decision context while allowing new people to question inherited assumptions.
- **Methodological objective:** Enable institutional continuity without substituting documentation for judgment or continuity for rigidity.
- **Limitations / conditions of use:** Not all knowledge is transferable or suitable for recording. Confidentiality and appropriate access must be respected. This contribution is an analytical approach from the article, not a separately approved checklist or formal procedure.
- **Relationship to existing SARGEN™ methodology:** Extends institutional memory and continuity concerns into human roles, responsibility transfer, and the governance of accumulated learning.
- **Cross-links:** [Publications](publications.md) — PUB-0031; [Identity](identity.md) — PUB-0031; [Philosophy](philosophy.md) — PUB-0031.
- **Open questions or review notes:** Do not treat this contribution record as a new LOCKED methodology without separate approval.
